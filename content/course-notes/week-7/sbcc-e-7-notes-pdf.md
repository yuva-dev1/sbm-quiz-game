The Great lineage of King Parikshith

7.  Great Lineage of King Parikshith

Chapter 07 continues
Suta  Pauranika  next  started  the  story  of  Parikshith,  which  serves  as  a  prelude  to
Krishna Katha. Srimad Bhagavatam begins where Mahabharata ends. At the end of
the Mahabharata war, there were very few survivors on sides of both the Kauravas
and  the  Pandavas.  Ashwatthama,  the  son  of  Dronacharya,  sought  to  avenge  the
death  of  his  father.  Against  the  wish  of  even  Duryodhana  he  set  out  to  kill  the
Pandavas  in  the  middle  of  the  night,  but  ended  up  killing  their  five  sons  instead  -
the Upapandavas. Waking the next morning only to see the headless bodies of her
five  sons,  Draupadi  screamed  and  was  inconsolable.  The  Pandavas  and  Lord
Krishna rushed to the tent of Draupadi and were shocked beyond words to witness
the gory scene. Arjuna vowed to bring to her the head of the perpetrator. Knowing
that it must have been none other than Ashwatthama, Arjuna and Lord Krishna set
out to catch him, who was by then running for his life. Frightened and cornered by
Arjuna  and  Krishna,  Ashwatthama,  in  desperation,  hurled  at  them  the  powerful
Brahmastra,  which  he  did  not  know  to  properly  control  or  wield.  Arjuna
surrendered  to  the  Lord,  seeking  His  guidance.  Bhagavan  instructed  Arjuna  to
counter  it  with  another  Brahmastra  and  retract  both  of  them.  Arjuna  did  so,  and
then bound Ashwatthama.
Lord Krishna tested Arjuna by asking him to kill Ashwatthama then and there. But
knowing  the  heart  of  Krishna,  Arjuna  refrained  from  killing  him  and  merely
dragged the bound Ashwatthama to the tent of Draupadi. Draupadi was shocked to
see that the perpetrator was Ashwatthama. Empathising with the pain of his mother
Krupi,  the  compassionate  Draupadi  requested  that  he  be  released.  Yudhishthira
agreed,  while  Bhima  countered  it.  Arjuna  looked  to  Krishna  again  and  Bhagavan
said,  “Do  that  which  would  please  all  the  parties  and  Me!”  Arjuna  insulted
Ashwatthama by cutting off the gem in his head and then let him go. The Pandavas
performed the funeral rites for their sons and relatives.

Chapter 08
Krishna was now set to depart from Hastinapura. While he was about to take leave
of everyone, Uttara, the pregnant wife of Abhimanyu, came running to Krishna and
surrendered. An insulted and angry Ashwatthama had hurled more Brahmastras to
kill  the  child  in  her  womb  and  all  the  Pandavas.  Bhagavan  protected  all  of  them.
When Queen Kunti witnessed this, a beautiful soulful stuti, a song of praise, poured
forth  from  her.  Recognizing  her  nephew  as  the  primal  Lord  of  this  universe,  she
glorified Lord Krishna. (26 verses)

Kunti  Devi  listed  all  the  times  in  her  life  when  Bhagavan  protected  her  and  her
family,  and  said,  “O  Krishna!  May  such  calamities  befall  me  incessantly  so  that  I
would  have  your  darshan  always!”  She  prayed  that  her  mind  dwell  constantly  in
Bhagavan  and  asked  Him  to  stay  longer  in  Hastinapura.  Lord  Krishna  too
acquiesced and stayed back.

Chapter 09
King  Yudhishthira  refused  to  be  crowned,  as  he  felt  guilty  about  the  death  of  his
kinsmen.  In  order  to  bring  clarity  to  Yudhishthira,  Bhagavan  Krishna  took
Pandavas  and  Draupadi  to  the  battlefield  where  Bhishmacharya  lay  on  a  bed  of
arrows.  Bhishmacharya  was  moved  to  see  the  Pandavas  and  Draupadi,  as  he
recollected  the  hardships  that  they  had  gone  through.  He  instructed  King
Yudhishthira  in  all  the  dharmas  and  finally  expanded  the  Bhagavata  Dharma  of
Nama sankirtan in the form of Vishnu Sahasranama. He then focused his mind on
Bhagavan Sri Krishna alone and extolled Him in a beautiful stuti (11 verses).

In every verse he prayed for divine love. He recollected the special grace of Lord
Krishna in the battlefield where Bhagavan gave up His vow to not wield a weapon,
in  order  to  uphold  the  vow  of  Bhishmacharya  to  make  Him  wield  one.
Bhishmacharya finally remembered with great reverence the love that the Gopis of
Vrindavan had for Lord Krishna, and gave up his mortal coil.

Chapter 10 & 11
Bhagavan took leave of all at Hastinapuram and returned to Dwaraka, to the joy of
His subjects and family in Dwaraka, and the sorrow of the Pandavas.

Chapter 12
Sage Shaunaka then asked Suta Pauranika about the story of Parikshith, who was
saved by the Lord while he was in the womb of his mother, Uttara. As he was being
scorched  by  the  fire  of  Ashwatthama’s  Brahmastra,  the  baby  in  the  womb  beheld
the wonderful form of the Lord in front of him, swirling His mace and quenching
the  fire.  The  child  was  then  born  to  Uttara  on  an  auspicious  day,  delighting
everyone.
When his horoscope was sketched, the brahmanas narrated the entire future of the
child—that he would be a great king, that he would be cursed to die in seven days,
and that he would ultimately attain Sri Krishna’s lotus feet.

Since  he  was  protected  and  virtually  ‘given’  by  the  Lord  he  was  called
‘Vishnuraatha’ and because he kept searching for that wonderful form of the Lord
that he beheld while in his mother’s womb, he was called Parikshith.

Krishna  came  to  Hastinapura  for  the  Ashwamedha  sacrifice  conducted  by
Yudhishthira.  After  completing  three  Ashwamedha  sacrifices,  Krishna  returned  to
Dwaraka. Arjuna accompanied Krishna to Dwaraka this time.

Chapter 13
Vidura  returned  to  Hastinapura  after  having  ascertained  the  truth  about  the  Self
from Sage Maitreya. Yudhishthira and his brothers and all other relatives were very
happy  to  see  him  back.  Yudhishthira  enquired  about  the  travels  of  Vidura  and
about the Yadavas. Vidura described everything in detail leaving out the destruction
of Yadava kula (which he had heard about earlier from Uddhava), for he could not
bring himself to break the bad news and see them suffer.
Vidura waited for an opportune moment and counseled his brother Dhritarashtra,
inspiring him to seek the ultimate truth about the Self. Dhritarashtra, Gandhari and
Vidura  left  Hastinapura  without  informing  anyone.  Finding  them  gone  one
morning,  a  pained  Yudhishthira  was  consoled  by  Sage  Narada.  Sage  Narada  told
him they had gone to the ashram of sages in the southern Himalayas where Ganga
Devi  flows  as  seven  streams,  called  Saptasrota,  and  how,  having  witnessed  both
Dhritarashtra and Gandhari give up their mortal coil, Vidura would go out again on
a pilgrimage.

Chapter 14 & 15
Arjuna had gone to Dwaraka to see his relatives and had not returned even though
many  months  had  passed.  Seeing  ill  omens  everywhere,  Yudhishthira  began  to
worry and spoke about his apprehensions to Bhima. While he was conversing with
Bhima, a dejected Arjuna returned. Seeing his face dark with sorrow Yudhishthira
pressed him to learn what was wrong.
Arjuna  broke  down  and  revealed  the  fate  of  the  Yadavas,  who  while  in  an
intoxicated  state  fought  among  themselves,  impelled  by  the  curse  of  sages,  and
destroyed their own entire race. He recollected all the times Lord Krishna had been
with  the  Pandavas,  guiding  them  and  protecting  them.  He  realized  that  all  his
prowess was Lord Krishna’s grace and not his own.
Kunti Devi gave up her body the moment she heard that Bhagavan had completed
His  leela  on  earth.  Yudhishthira  installed  Parikshith  as  the  king,  and  all  the
Pandavas followed by Draupadi left the kingdom, reached the Himalayas, and shed
their body. Vidura too left his mortal coil in Prabhasa kshetra.

Chapter 16 & 17
The  noble  king  Parikshith  ruled  ably  over  the  earth.  He  married  King  Uttara
Kumara’s  daughter  Iravati  and  begot  four  sons.  Once,  during  his  conquests,  he
beheld a strange divine vision. He saw a cow that was shedding tears profusely as it
stood alongside a bull who was standing on only one leg, with its other three legs
having  been  broken.  The  bull  was  enquiring  as  to  why  the  cow  was  in  tears.  The
cow revealed that it was because the Lord had left her. Parikshith then saw a man
who looked like a rogue but who was disguised as a king, hurting the cow and bull.
Parikshith  addressed  the  man  angrily  and  asked  who  he  was,  and  also  asked  the
bull about who had cut off his three legs.

The four legs of Dharma are:
1. Tapas - austerity
2. Shaucham - purity
3. Daya - compassion
4. Satyam - truth.
Except satyam, all the other limbs of dharma (the bull) were broken.

The  bull  did  not  point  any  fingers  or  blame  the  man,  even  though  it  was  obvious
that  it  was  he  who  had  hurt  the  bull.  Parikshith  then  understood  the  bull  to  be
Dharma,  the  cow  to  be  Mother  Earth,  and  the  man  posing  as  a  king  to  be  Kali
Purusha. (When we gossip about the sinful activities of another, we get a portion of that sin.
Dharma avoided that! Hence Parikshith could discern that the bull was Dharma.)

When  Parikshith  was  about  to  kill  Kali  Purusha,  Kali  Purusha  fell  at  his  feet  and
surrendered.  Thus  Parikshith  refrained  from  killing  him,  and  provided  him  with
four  places  to  reside  -  gambling,  alcohol,  adultery,  and  violence  (falsehood,
intoxication, lust, and cruelty). When Kali Purusha requested for one more place -
Parikshith  gave  ‘gold’  (rajoguna)  as  the  5th  place  for  Kali  Purusha  to  stay.
Parikshith  then  restored  the  three  legs  of  Dharma  and  continued  to  rule  his
kingdom well.

Parikshith did not kill Kali Purusha and merely subdued him, even though this Kali
was literally a storehouse of evils, because he knew that in the Kali yuga, everyone
could  attain  Bhagavan  easily  through  the  path  of  Namasankirtana.  Another
‘positive aspect’ of the age of Kali is that good intentions, even if not translated to
action,  are  rewarded,  while  bad  intentions  are  not  punished  until  implemented
through action. Like a bee, Parikshith discerned this nectar (true essence) from the
flower (Kaliyuga) and let it be.
